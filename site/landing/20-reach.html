Why it matters

Reach

Short plans, short applies

Each record names the resources it depends on. A plan starts at your change and follows those dependencies for as long as a value changes. It looks at what the change can touch and leaves the rest alone. You review a plan about your change, and the apply locks and writes only that.

What a plan reaches Refresh age

A staleness budget

Each record remembers when its provider was last asked, and the refresh age says how stale it may be before it is read again. A plan that looks at a record within its age does not ask again, so repeated plans cost fewer calls. A resource can set its own age as a standing promise, and a scheduled pravo drift --due reads the records that are past theirs, oldest first, for a cost you cap. The default age is zero: what a plan looks at is read.

Choosing a refresh age Both together

Calls follow your change

Reach decides what a plan looks at. The refresh age decides whether what it looks at is read, and how overdue a record must be before a scheduled sweep reads it. The calls to your providers follow the change and the ages you chose, not the number of resources in the state.

Cost follows the change For a team

One state for a large estate

Split an estate by ownership, permissions or blast radius, not to make plans fast. People and CI can apply to different parts of one state at the same time, because an apply locks only the resources it writes; an overlapping apply is refused, not queued. Drift is checked on a schedule with pravo drift, not on every plan.

When to split a state

The trade-off A resource that a plan does not reach is not read, so drift in it is found by pravo drift or by a plan that reaches it.

What grows with the estate pravo evaluates the whole specification and opens every record on each run, it reads every data source unless a refresh age is set, and a change that reaches every resource costs what the estate costs.
